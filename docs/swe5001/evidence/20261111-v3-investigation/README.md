The owner requested investigation before changing settings or running another workload, with zero drops and the unchanged p95 gate as the goal while demonstrating horizontal scale-out. This addendum does **not** repair the [failed r1/r2 campaigns](../20261111-load-reruns/README.md), alter their sealed evidence, claim event-relative latency acceptance or authorise another run. ## Conclusion: only part of the assumption is proven

**VU exhaustion and late scaling are established. Earlier scaling alone guaranteeing zero drops and acceptable p95 is not.** The data separates an early client/origin timing discrepancy from a later origin-side latency/scale-out transition. Service-average CPU hid newly added tasks near their CPU limit. That makes earlier capacity and task warm-up/traffic admission reasonable candidates for review, not a validated sole remedy. The unresolved early delay must be investigated rather than erased by a later good interval or a larger generator VU allowance.

## The early discrepancy must not be hand-waved away

At 08:25, 08:26 and 08:27 UTC, client HTTP-duration p95 was approximately 7.20 s, 10.00 s and 271 ms. In those same calendar minutes, retained ALB target-response **maximums**, not just p95s, were only 328, 640 and 348 ms. There were 4,306 non-200 client completions across these minutes. Existing CloudFront distribution-level 5xx/error metrics reported zero in those minutes, while later recording the 08:28 error burst. Distribution metrics include all paths/viewers and are not per-request joins.

These observations do not support blaming the initial symptom solely on slow application processing. Possible unobserved segments include the generator/host/network path, the edge and ALB phases before target timing starts, or requests not represented by a corresponding target-timing sample. The workstation observer also had its failed ECS API read at 08:26:18 during this interval; that is a useful correlation, not proof of one shared network cause. K6 retained 4,446 request-timeout warnings and two `unexpected EOF` warnings; no raw warning URLs or application payloads are published here.

In contrast, client and ALB target latency both rose sharply in 08:28–08:31, alongside newly added hot tasks. All 113 observed HTTP 503 responses have a matching aggregate target-side 5xx count in 08:28; aggregate equality alone is not a request-level trace or proof of which process returned them. The main drop burst and the early client-only discrepancy need not have the same cause.

## What the other reads establish—and what is missing

**R1 comparison is diagnostic, not a controlled attribution.** The same tool paired all 687,084 original HTTP records, including setup, and matched their original summary/raw hash. In r1's 06:27–06:28 UTC minutes, client p95 was near 10 s, service CPU was approximately 97–100%, and ALB target p95 was about 1.77–3.42 s. That supports the earlier r1 CPU-overload concern; it does not establish that r2's early client-only symptom has the same cause after task CPU doubled. The larger task and additional policy must not be credited with a proven independent benefit from this combined comparison.

- Existing Container Insights supplied 375 projected records: 30 service, 116 task and 229 container records. The source task reservation was 1024 CPU units; per-task percentages are calculated as `CpuUtilized / CpuReserved × 100`. Original-task and new-task values are kept separately, rather than treating service averages as every task's headroom.
- No unhealthy-host datapoint was nonzero during the queried workload. Empty error-only ALB series are reported as absent points, not silently converted into verified zeros. A healthy target is not proof of good business-response latency under cold load.
- Additional RDS/cache metrics did not show an instance-wide CPU/credit/I/O/cache-engine saturation explanation. Those aggregates and empty pool/cache-error filters do not establish per-request SQL latency, lock waits, Hikari wait time or absence of dependency stalls.
- The filtered commerce warning read returned ten unclassified startup-period matches and no recognized cache-unavailable, pool-wait, connection-limit, client-abort or OOM category. No raw messages/SQL/credentials were retained. Unclassified matches are not silently relabelled benign, and absence of logged failures is not absence of waiting.
- Post-run readback found CloudFront and ALB access logging disabled, no additional CloudFront monitoring subscription, and no historical `OriginLatency` points. This is an instrumentation gap, not zero origin latency. No instrumentation was enabled by this investigation.

## Recommended next review—not executed

1. **Instrument the missing boundaries first.** Review private, access-restricted ALB/edge timing logs or equivalent request correlation, plus task-level HTTP concurrency, request queue/thread metrics, JVM/GC and Hikari wait metrics. Retain only approved non-secret projections; do not publish raw application/identity payloads or secret-bearing configuration. Logging cannot recover missing historical traces retroactively.
2. **Remove the unresolved workstation/ISP variable.** Review a dedicated, adequately sized Singapore AWS generator with CPU/memory/network capture, preserving driver, workload, VU bounds and gates. Its placement is a disclosed generator variant, not proof that the old capture's network was healthy. No new generator was provisioned here.
3. **Review earlier horizontal capacity and real warm readiness together.** Keep one fixed moderate commerce shape and the one-task V3 start. Calibrate the leading trigger against a measured healthy one-commerce-task reference, alarm delay and usable task startup; inspect whether newly admitted tasks need representative warm-up before receiving the full share. Preserve health dependencies, pool/timeout settings and the zero-drop/p95 gates. Do not claim that merely changing CPU 50% to another arbitrary number is a proven fix.
4. **Only then authorise a fresh measured variant.** It must show actual automatic scale-out with full delivered work, zero drops and p95 <800 ms outside the permitted three-minute windows. Raising VUs/timeouts, starting with several tasks or selecting only recovered minutes cannot establish the original scenario. The independent 80%-linearity criterion still requires its own comparable measurements.

## Tooling and preservation

The timing diagnostic validated all seven component means/p95s against the untouched original summary and checked the complete raw SHA-256. TLS/connect durations are subsets of blocked time and were not added twice. Five repeated timestamp/tag keys in r2 were preserved as distinct strictly ordered exported metric batches, not deduplicated requests. The first diagnostic stopped on its mistaken global-uniqueness requirement; that failure and source are retained. Seven offline unit tests cover interpolation, nanosecond parsing through reconstruction, repeated batches, refusal of ambiguous ordering and preservation of negative timing measurements. These are diagnostic-tool tests, not application acceptance.

The interval reconstruction is not exact independent concurrency telemetry: r1's inferred peak was 1,001 while its actual retained VU gauge remained capped at 1,000. This one-request overlap is a reconstruction/timing consistency limit, not evidence of another VU or permission to raise the cap. R1 retained one negative receiving-phase value; r2 retained four negative sending-phase values. All were preserved, not clamped or filtered. R2's reconstructed 1,000 peak agrees with its VU gauge and drop timing, but the approximately 84-ms separation must not be presented as a precisely measured causal latency.

Original campaign manifests, raw samples, summaries, collector failures and normalizer refusal remain unchanged. The original staged index and stashes are preserved. This is an evidence addendum only; no new performance pass, root-cause certainty, board closure or Git publication is claimed.
